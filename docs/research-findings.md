# Research findings (summary for design and prompts)

Source of truth: the "Why People Dislike Writing — Research Summary" doc, which has full citations.

## Why people dislike writing

1. **Cognitive overload:** planning, translating ideas into words, and reviewing compete for limited working memory (Kellogg). Doing all three at once overloads novices.
2. **Fear of judgment, low confidence:** writing apprehension leads to avoidance; confidence (self-efficacy) shapes anxiety and performance.
3. **Rigid rules and perfectionism:** blocked writers follow rigid rules ("the intro must be perfect"); unblocked writers have few, flexible rules (Rose). Everyone finds writing hard.
4. **No choice, no real reader:** enjoyment falls through school; autonomy and real purpose help.
5. **Feedback that doesn't teach:** students find comments unhelpful when vague, without guidance, only negative, or unrelated to criteria (Weaver).

Symptom: procrastination (80–95% of students). Native speakers struggle with thinking and structure, not grammar. Non-native writers carry extra language load and write in shorter bursts. Low-confidence writers are the most likely to hand writing off to AI.

## What works

1. **Teach strategies explicitly:** strategy instruction is among the largest effects on writing quality (0.82); SRSD also raises confidence.
2. **Separate the mental jobs:** outlining improved quality (Kellogg); the problem is editing while producing words (Elbow).
3. **Two valid routes:** high self-monitors do better drafting first; low self-monitors do better outlining first. Flexible beats strict step-by-step. Offer both.
4. **Revise big to small:** novices reword; experts restructure (Sommers).
5. **Write for a reader with a goal:** plain language's first rule; goal setting (0.70).
6. **Short, regular sessions** beat binges, though schedules vary.
7. **Expression help should make the user generate:** generating answers yourself improves long-term retention ("desirable difficulties").

## Genre structures (offer as optional guides, explain why each works)

| Genre | Structure |
| --- | --- |
| Argumentative essay | Thesis → claims → evidence → counterargument → conclusion |
| Scientific paper | IMRaD |
| Business email/memo | BLUF / Minto pyramid: conclusion first |
| Public information | Plain language: audience, needs-based order, headings |
| News | Inverted pyramid |
| Fiction / personal essay | Story arc; planners and discoverers both valid |

## Design implications

- Phases keep generation apart from polishing.
- AI review: top 3 issues, one specific strength, tied to the user's purpose or rubric, structure before sentences.
- Free write starts with "a messy first draft is expected".
- Templates are optional and explain themselves; never present them as rules.
- Track a writing-confidence check alongside error rates.
